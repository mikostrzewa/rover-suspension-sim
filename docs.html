1Overview#

The tool designs the coilover suspension of a rocker-leg rover in a 2D side view. Two legs hinge on one shared pivot O on the body. Each leg carries a wheel at its end and is held up by its own coilover, which runs from a mount on the body (mount 1) to a mount on the leg (mount 2).

It answers three questions in order: what spring the geometry needs (statics and sizing), how the rover behaves on real terrain and when dropped (multibody dynamics), and which spring rate, preload and damping give the smoothest ride for the centre of mass (tuner).

Typical workflow#

  1. Statics & sizing. Enter the mass, CG and leg geometry, or drag the points on the drawing. Pick how the spring is sized and check that the coilover fits the travel.
  2. Terrain run. Build a course from presets, obstacles, a freehand sketch or a measured CSV profile. Run it or drive it live, then read loads, travel and bottom-outs.
  3. Drop test. Drop the rover from a height, with pitch or forward speed, and find the highest drop that does not bottom the shocks.
  4. Tuner. Let the tool search spring rate, preload and damping that minimise acceleration at the CG over the run and the drop.
  5. Presets. Save each design you want to compare and switch between them from the sidebar.

Units and sign conventions#

  • Inputs use mm, kg, N, degrees; spring rates in N/mm; damping in N·s/m (force per metre per second of shock shaft speed). The dynamics runs internally in SI units.
  • Left β in the statics drawing is the rear leg and Right α the front leg; the rover drives to the right (+x).
  • Leg angle θ is measured below the body horizontal, outward from the pivot.
  • Wheel travel h is vertical movement of the axle relative to the level body: + is bump (wheel up), − is droop.
  • Pitch φ is + nose up. Coilover force is + when it pushes the eyes apart (normal compression load).
  • Load factor is the CG’s vertical acceleration plus gravity, in g: 1 g at rest.

Where your work is kept#

Everything stays in your browser on this computer. The current design (geometry, map and every setting) is saved automatically as you edit, so the page reopens where you left it. Presets and the screen layout are saved separately. Clearing the browser’s site data erases them, so keep a copy with Download JSON (Statics tab) or Export presets (sidebar).

2Geometry#

All geometry is defined in the body frame: origin at the pivot O, x outward towards the wheel of the leg in question, y up. The two legs are described the same way and mirrored, so the same numbers give a symmetric rover.

For a leg at angle θ, with side sign s = +1 for the front leg and −1 for the rear leg:

axle   b(θ) = ( s·L·cos θ , −L·sin θ )
mount 2   P2(θ) = ( s·(a·cos θ + e·sin θ) , −a·sin θ + e·cos θ )
mount 1   P1 = ( s·x1 , y1 )
spring length   ℓ(θ) = |P2 − P1| ,   unit vector   û = (P2 − P1) / ℓ

Wheel travel moves the axle vertically while the body stays level, which fixes the leg angle at any travel:

sin θ(h) = sin θ0 − h / L(2.1)

The spring compression from ride is Δ(h) = ℓ(0) − ℓ(h). A leg cannot reach a travel where |sin θ| would exceed 1; the tool flags that as a geometry problem.

3Statics model#

The statics treat one side of the rover with the body held level and nothing moving. The wheels roll freely, so the ground pushes straight up at each contact point directly below the axle. In the statics all of the weight is placed at the CG; leg and wheel masses are added in the dynamics.

Wheel loads#

Fw = m·g·share(3.1)
FN,front = Fw·(xcg − Xrear) / (Xfront − Xrear) ,   FN,rear = Fw − FN,front(3.2)

Here X are the axle x positions in the body frame (the rear one is negative). With the CG over the pivot and a symmetric rover each wheel carries half: 122.6 N for the default 50 kg rover at 50 % share.

Spring force, leverage and motion ratio#

Taking moments about the pivot removes the unknown pin force:

FN·xw + Fs·(P2 × û) = 0   ⇒   Fs = FN·IR ,   IR = xw / d⊥(3.3)

xw is the horizontal distance from the pivot to the axle and d⊥ is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the coilover line. IR (the leverage, or installation ratio) says how many newtons the spring must push for each newton at the wheel. The pin reaction follows from the force balance of the leg:

R = −( FN·ŷ + Fs·û )(3.4)

By virtual work, Fs·dΔ = FN·dh, so the motion ratio (spring travel per unit wheel travel) is the inverse of the leverage:

MR = dΔ/dh = 1 / IR(3.5)

Default design at ride: xw = 563.8 mm, d⊥ = 299.4 mm, IR = 1.883, MR = 0.531, spring force 230.9 N.

Why statics alone cannot pick the spring rate. The force needed to hold the rover level, Fs(h) = IR(h)·FN, barely changes across the travel for this geometry (231 N at ride, about 230 N at +100 mm). Any spring rate can hold the rover at its design angle once the preload is right, so a second condition is needed to choose k. That is what the sizing methods below provide. Note also that a slow rock in this 2D side view mostly tilts the body like a seesaw; the springs compress mainly in dynamic hits and in 3D warp, which the dynamics tabs capture.

4Spring sizing#

Ride height is always the anchor: at the design angle θ0 the spring must hold the static wheel load,

F0 = IR(0)·FN0 ,   preload   x0 = F0 / k(4.1)

where x0 is how far the spring is compressed from its free length at ride. The spring rate k then comes from the method you choose:

MethodConditionSpring rate
Droop travel, no preload (default)The spring just reaches its free length (zero force) with the wheel at full droop.k = F0 / (ℓdroop − ℓ0)
Load at max bumpAt max bump the wheel load is G × the static load.k = (G·FN0·IRbump − F0) / (ℓ0 − ℓbump)
Ride frequencyEach corner bounces at f on its wheel rate.k = (2πf)²·mc / MR0² ,   mc = FN0/g
Spring rate I chooseYou give the rate.k as entered

Default: droop sizing gives k = 230.9 N / 26.8 mm = 8.62 N/mm, with 26.8 mm of preload.

Coilover fit#

The shock’s compressed length is extended length − stroke. The tool checks that the ride length lies between the two, that the shock does not bottom before max bump (it reports the travel where it would) and does not top out before max droop. Stroke use is (ℓdroop − ℓbump) / stroke.

6Dynamics model#

The Terrain run, Drop test and Tuner tabs share one multibody model of one side of the rover moving in its vertical plane.

Coordinates and masses#

Generalized coordinatesq = ( x, y, φ, θrear, θfront ): pivot position, body pitch and the two leg angles
Wheel statesspin ω and angle of each wheel (angle only for drawing)
Bodymb = m·share − 2(mw + ml) at the CG, pitch inertia Ib = mb·ky²
Each leguniform rod of mass ml from O to the axle, I = ml·L²/12 about its middle
Each wheel endpoint mass mw at the axle (wheel, tyre, motor) plus spin inertia J

Equations of motion#

Every mass point p = O + R(φ)·b(q) has velocity v = Jp·q̇. Lagrange’s equations give

M(q)·q̈ = Q − Σk mk·JkT·avp,k ,   M = Σk mk·JkTJk + rotational inertias(6.1)

where avp is the centripetal and Coriolis part of each point’s acceleration (for an axle: R·(−bφ̇² + 2·S·b′·φ̇θ̇ − bθ̇²), S = 90° rotation). The 5×5 system is solved at every evaluation. The generalized forces Q collect:

ForceContribution
GravityJkT·(0, −mkg) for every mass
CoiloverF·∂ℓ/∂θ in that leg’s row (and the equal and opposite moment on the body)
Tyre contactJaxleT·Fc: the axle carries the contact force
Motorreaction torque −T on the leg it is bolted to; +T spins the wheel

Coilover#

F = max(0, k·(x0 + ℓ0 − ℓ))  +  Fdamper  +  Fstop(6.2)
Fdamper = −cbump·ℓ̇   (compressing, ℓ̇ < 0)   or   −crebound·ℓ̇   (extending)(6.3)

Below the compressed length the bump stop adds kbs·(ℓmin − ℓ) plus 2500 N·s/m of damping while compressing; beyond full extension the top-out stop pulls back the same way. Damping is linear and measured at the shock shaft.

Preload in the dynamics#

The dynamics carries the leg and wheel weight directly through the tyres, so the spring holds less than in the statics. The preload is re-solved so the rover rests exactly at θ0: static wheel loads come from the centroid of all masses, then

Fspring = IR·( FN − (mw + ml/2)·g ) ,   x0 = Fspring / k(6.4)

Default rover: 175.5 N and 20.4 mm of preload, against 230.9 N and 26.8 mm in the statics. The spring rate itself always comes from the Statics tab.

Tyre contact, friction and rolling resistance#

The tyre is a circle of radius r pressed into the ground (a penalty contact). The ground is a polyline sampled every 5 mm; every local minimum of distance from the axle that is closer than r is a contact patch, so a wheel on a step edge gets two patches (edge and floor), up to three in all.

δ = r − d ,   N = max(0, kt·δ + ct·δ̇)(6.5)
Ft = −μ·N·tanh(s / vε) ,   vε = 0.05 m/s(6.6)
Mrr = Crr·N·r·tanh(ω / 0.5 rad/s)(6.7)

s is the speed of the tyre surface relative to the ground at the contact, so friction is Coulomb friction smoothed over ±0.05 m/s. Normal force acts from the contact point towards the axle.

Rigid tyre (default): kt = 2000 N/mm and ct = 2·0.9·√(kt·mw), about 4025 N·s/m for 2.5 kg, giving roughly 0.06 mm of deflection at rest. Compliant tyre: your own kt and ct.

Rigid tyres and peak loads. A rigid wheel hitting a rock stops in about a millisecond, and the instantaneous force (thousands of newtons) depends mostly on the assumed contact stiffness. The tool therefore reports peaks as 10 ms moving averages of interval-averaged channels. For realistic impact peaks, switch to the compliant model with your measured tyre stiffness.

Motors and wheel spin#

T = clamp( Tstall·(u − ωrel / ω0) , ±Tstall )(6.8)
J·ω̇ = T − τcontact − Mrr(6.9)

A brushed-DC-style torque curve per driven wheel: throttle u from −1 to 1, ωrel is the wheel speed relative to the leg the motor is bolted to, ω0 the no-load speed. At zero throttle, Brake keeps the curve with u = 0 (the motor resists rotation) and Coast gives no torque.

Integration and recording#

  • Fourth-order Runge–Kutta with an adaptive step that keeps the stiff terms stable: h = clamp(min(0.85/λf, 1/√(kt/mw), mw/ct), 15 µs, 0.4 ms), with λf = μ·Nmax/vε·(r²/J + 1/mw).
  • Samples are recorded every 5 ms (1 ms in the drop test). Wheel loads, coilover forces and the load factor are also recorded as averages over each sample interval, integrated with the same RK4 weights as the motion, so short impacts keep their true impulse, like a data logger with an anti-alias filter.
  • The load factor is the CG’s acceleration from the solved equations: nz = (ay + g)/g (1 g at rest), nx = ax/g.

Starting states#

  • Terrain run: legs at θ0, body level, tyres pressed in by their static load, pivot at the start position, moving at the initial speed with the wheels rolling to match.
  • Drop test: body at the release pitch, each leg hanging where its coilover balances the leg’s own weight, lowest tyre at the drop height above flat ground.

8Terrain run tab#

The ground#

Ground height is a smooth curve through your control points plus the shape of every obstacle. The curve is a monotone cubic spline (Fritsch–Carlson), so it never overshoots between points: a flat stretch stays flat and a step does not ring. The ground is flat beyond the first and last point, and is sampled every 5 mm for contact.

Editing the map#

  • Switch to Edit map. With the Move tool, drag the blue control points; double-click empty ground to add a point and double-click a point to delete it.
  • Obstacles show two diamonds: drag the top one to move the obstacle (position and height), the side one to change its width. The + chips add an obstacle in the middle of the view (the middle of the map when not editing); the list below lets you type x, h, w and n, and × removes one.
  • Drag the green flag to set the start. Drag empty space to pan; use − and + to zoom; Fit map shows it all. Height × exaggerates heights on screen only.
  • The Draw tool sketches a new ground profile over the stretch you draw. The stroke is thinned and simplified (3 mm tolerance) and replaces the control points in that range.
  • Import CSV profile reads two numeric columns, distance and height, separated by commas, semicolons, tabs or spaces; header and other non-numeric lines are skipped. With auto units, a profile spanning less than 300 is read as metres, otherwise millimetres. It is shifted to start at 0, points closer than 5 mm are merged, very long profiles are simplified to 1 mm, and it must be at least 1 m long. It replaces the ground curve; obstacles stay on top.
  • Flatten ground and Remove obstacles clear one or the other.

Results#

  • Replay: play, pause, scrub the timeline or click a chart or the minimap to jump; playback speed 0.25× to 2×. The force arrows show tyre and coilover forces.
  • Readouts at the replay time: speed, throttle, pitch and load factor, and for each wheel the load, coilover travel from ride (with a bar across its stroke), coilover force (marked when on a stop), motor torque and slip.
  • Tiles: result, average speed, peak wheel load (also as a multiple of static), peak coilover force, closest approach to the bump stop (negative means it went into the stop), and peak load factor with maximum pitch and the energy the motors delivered.
  • Checks: how often and how long each coilover hit its bump stop, time at full extension, and time each wheel spent off the ground (over 20 ms).
  • Charts: speed, wheel loads, coilover length (with the shock limits), coilover force, load factor at the CG, body pitch, motor torque and wheel slip, front and rear.
  • Download run CSV: one row per 5 ms sample with time, position, pivot height, pitch, speed, throttle, load factor, wheel loads, coilover lengths and forces (also split into spring, damper and stop), motor torques, slip, motor power, and the interval-averaged wheel loads, coilover forces and load factor.

  • Tiles: peak load factor (10 ms average) and when it happened, peak wheel load, peak coilover force, closest approach to the bump stop, peak bump-stop force, and settling time (from touchdown until the load factor stays within ±0.05 g).

Find max drop height#

Searches 0–2000 mm by bisection for the highest drop that does not touch the bump stops: up to 11 halvings, or until the bracket is under 5 mm. Each trial runs for the fall time plus 0.6 s (0.5–1.2 s) and stops early at the first bump-stop contact. The answer is rounded down with a 3 mm margin, then simulated and shown.

10Tuner tab#

The tuner searches for the spring rate, preload and damping that make the ride smoothest at the centre of mass, using the same dynamics as the other tabs. Every trial drives the chosen scenarios with one setup and is scored against your current setup.

Front and rear always get the same values. Untick a variable to keep it at its current value.

Preload as ride height. Instead of a raw preload, the tuner moves the ride height by dh (+ = sits lower). Each leg’s start angle becomes θ′ = asin(sin θ0 − dh/L) and the preload is solved so the rover rests level exactly there (eq. 6.4). This keeps every candidate physically consistent. Max bump and droop shift with it so the shock limits stay where they are. The tuner reports the matching collar preload, the spring compression with the shock fully extended:

collar preload = x0 + ℓride − ℓextended   (negative: the spring is loose at full extension)(10.1)

So the current setup scores exactly 1.00 and 0.90 means 10 % better. Penalties are added to a scenario’s term: +1 + (shortfall/10 mm) if the coilovers come closer to the bump stop than the clearance you set, and +10 if the run fails (stall, tip-over, unstable) where the current setup did not.

Search#

  1. Simulate the current setup (the reference).
  2. A Latin-hypercube scan of max(6, 2·variables + 2) setups spread evenly across the ranges.
  3. A bounded Nelder–Mead simplex from the best setup so far (reflection, expansion, contraction and shrink, starting simplex 15 % of each range), until the trial budget is used or the simplex is smaller than 0.4 % of the ranges.

The inputs are copied when you press Start, so editing the design during a run does not change it; the results are marked as out of date afterwards.

Getting useful answers. With rigid tyres a rock strike goes straight through the legs into the pivot, so the suspension can do little about those peaks; enter your real tyre stiffness before trusting terrain results. If a tuned value sits at the edge of its range, widen the range and run again. The clearance limit keeps the tuner from buying comfort with bottom-outs.

14Assumptions and limits#

  • 2D, one side. Only the side view is modelled, carrying a share of the weight. Roll, chassis twist, left-right load transfer and 3D warp are not included.
  • Rigid parts. Body and legs are rigid, joints have no play or bushing compliance, and both legs pivot on one axis.
  • Linear coilover. One spring rate, linear bump and rebound damping, no position- or speed-sensitive valving; the stops are stiff linear springs.
  • Simple tyre. A circle of zero width on a hard surface. No sinkage, sand or regolith behaviour (no terramechanics), no tyre carcass dynamics beyond the radial spring and damper.
  • Simple motors. A linear torque–speed line with no current, voltage, thermal or gear-backlash limits.
  • No body–ground contact. The body can pass through obstacles; watch the drawing for clearance.
  • Statics put all weight at the CG and assume the body is held level; the dynamics corrects the preload for leg and wheel masses.
  • Default numbers were eyeballed from early CAD with a 600 mm leg. Replace them with measured values before drawing conclusions.
